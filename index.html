About the math

Two methods answer the same question on this page. One counts, one samples. Everything below assumes a 365-day year with birthdays spread uniformly and independently — the standard idealisation.

The question

Fix a group of M people and a threshold N. We want the probability that some day of the year is the birthday of at least N of them. Note what this is not: it is not about a particular date, and it is not about a particular group of N people. Any day will do, and any N of the M will do. That is exactly why the answer is so much larger than intuition suggests.

Assigning M people to 365 days gives 365^M equally likely outcomes. Both methods below are just two ways of asking how many of those outcomes contain a clash.

Method 1 — counting, exactly

Direct counting is awkward, because a group can clash on several days at once and those cases would be double-counted. So we count the complement instead: arrangements in which every day holds fewer than N people. Then

P(at least N share) = 1 − P(no day holds N or more)

The complement is a classic exponential-generating-function count. A single day that receives fewer than N people contributes the truncated series 1 + x + x²/2! + … + x^(N-1)/(N-1)!, where the coefficient of x^k carries the 1/k! that accounts for the people on that day being interchangeable. Because the 365 days are filled independently, the whole year is that series raised to the 365th power. Reading off the coefficient of x^M and multiplying by M! turns it back into a count of labelled people:

P(no day holds N or more) = M!/365M · [xM] (1 + x + x²/2! + … + xN-1/(N-1)!)365

where [x^M] means "the coefficient of x^M". The page multiplies that polynomial out by repeated squaring, so it costs about log₂(365) ≈ 9 multiplications rather than 365.

Sanity check. At N = 2 the series is just 1 + x, and (1+x)^365 has coefficient C(365, M). Times M! that is 365 · 364 · … · (365−M+1), the familiar formula for the ordinary birthday problem. The general version is the same idea with a longer series.

One numerical wrinkle, if your teacher is the sort to ask. Raised to the 365th power those coefficients span hundreds of orders of magnitude, and the smallest ones vanish in floating point — which would silently report certainty where there is none. The page avoids it by tilting the series first: rescaling it into a genuine probability distribution (a truncated Poisson) so the coefficients become probabilities between 0 and 1, largest exactly where the interesting group sizes are. The tilt is undone afterwards in logarithms, so the answer is unchanged — only the arithmetic is made safe.

The results match the known sequence for the smallest group giving even odds — 23, 88, 187, 313, 460, 623, 798, 985, 1181 people for thresholds 2 through 10.

Method 2 — sampling (Monte Carlo)

The other approach ignores the counting entirely and just runs the experiment. One trial: draw a birthday for each of the M people, uniformly from 1 to 365, independently; then check whether any single day collected N or more. That is one yes-or-no outcome. Repeat Z times and report the fraction that hit:

p̂ = (trials that hit) / Z

Each trial is a Bernoulli(p) variable, so the number of hits is Binomial(Z, p). That makes p̂ an unbiased estimator — E[p̂] = p — with variance p(1−p)/Z and standard error

SE = √( p(1−p) / Z )

The law of large numbers is why p̂ converges to p at all; the central limit theorem is why the error is approximately normal, which is where the ±1.96·SE band on the chart comes from. That band is drawn as a funnel and the estimate wanders inside it.

The important consequence is the shape of that funnel. Error falls like 1/√Z, so ten thousand trials are only ten times better than a hundred, and buying one more decimal digit costs a hundred times the work. At 1,000 trials the estimate is worth about ±3 points; at 1,000,000 it is worth about ±0.1. This is the single most useful thing on the page: it is the reason exact methods are worth the trouble whenever they exist.

Where the exact method stops, and why

This page computes exactly up to 10 people sharing a day and switches to simulation above that. That boundary is a practical budget, not a mathematical wall — the formula above is valid for every N, and nothing about it breaks at 11.

What breaks is the time budget. The polynomial's degree has to reach the size of the group, and the cost grows with the square of that. Past a threshold of 10, the group sizes where the answer stops being approximately zero run into the thousands — a threshold of 11 needs about 1,385 people for even odds, 12 needs about 1,596 — so the exact pass gets slow enough to be felt on a page that recalculates as you type. Sampling has no such scaling problem, so it takes over.

Worth knowing: high thresholds are unlikely in small groups by a wide margin. Getting 15 people onto one day needs a crowd of several thousand before it stops being a once-in-a-lifetime event, and below that a simulation will honestly report zero hits out of every trial.

The assumptions, and how wrong they are

Real birthdays are not uniform. There is seasonal clustering, a weekday effect from scheduled deliveries, and a dip around 29 February. Any departure from uniformity increases the chance of a coincidence — the uniform case is the one that spreads people out the most — so every figure here is a mild underestimate of the real world. Leap days are not modelled at all, which pushes very slightly the other way. Both effects are small next to the counter-intuitive size of the answer itself.
